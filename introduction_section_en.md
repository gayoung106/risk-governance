# Introduction

## 1. Introduction

Governments increasingly rely on the collection, sharing, and use of personal information, including location data, medical information, and contact records, to respond rapidly to infectious disease outbreaks, natural hazards, and other social disasters. Such use of personal information is a central instrument of disaster data governance, and its scope and frequency continue to expand. As governments rely more heavily on personal information in disaster response, it becomes increasingly important to understand whether citizens accept such use. Policy acceptance therefore deserves careful attention in the design of disaster data governance, because the practical sustainability of data use depends on citizens' willingness to accept it.

The use of personal information in disasters, however, lies at the intersection of two potentially competing values: public safety and privacy protection. Disaster response agencies may improve public safety by using personal information quickly, yet such use also entails risks, including data leakage and secondary use beyond the original purpose. Under these conditions, citizens' policy acceptance is not determined solely by legal permissibility. It may also depend on how citizens perceive risk and how they evaluate the actors responsible for data management. Identifying the perceptual factors associated with policy acceptance is therefore a continuing issue in the design of disaster data governance.

Prior research offers two broad lines of explanation. First, research on privacy calculus argues that individuals decide whether to disclose information by weighing the risks and benefits of doing so (Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005; Dinev & Hart, 2006). From this perspective, higher risk perception should be associated with lower willingness to provide information. Second, research on trust argues that, when individuals cannot directly verify all relevant information, trust functions as a mechanism that enables judgment (Luhmann, 1979; Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995). From this perspective, higher trust in the managing actor should be associated with greater willingness to cooperate with policy. Existing studies have generally examined these perspectives separately, and relatively few have considered them jointly within a single analytical framework.

These two strands of research have not jointly examined whether judgments that data use by the managing actor is necessary and effective, and evaluations that personal information is currently being managed safely, have distinct pathways to policy acceptance. Nor have they examined whether these judgments are differently associated with policy acceptance depending on the level of risk perception. Building on this gap, the present study examines these questions in the context of disaster data governance.

Accordingly, this study addresses the following research question: How is policy acceptance of the use of personal information for disaster response associated with management trust, defined as judgments about the necessity and effectiveness of data use, and with positive perception of safe management, defined as an evaluation of the current management status, and how do these associations vary by the level of risk perception?

To answer this question, the study examines cross-sectional survey data from a general population sample in Korea (N = 1,094) concerning the use of personal information for disaster response. Policy acceptance was measured as the level of agreement with the collection, disclosure, and sharing of personal information. Management trust was measured as perceptions of the necessity and effectiveness of data use. Positive perception of safe management was measured as the perception that personal information is currently being managed safely. Risk perception was measured as the perceived possibility of leakage and misuse. Institutional trust was measured as general trust in government agencies responsible for disaster response. The analysis primarily uses OLS regression with HC3 robust standard errors, and also examines mediation, moderation, and information-type heterogeneity.

The findings are summarized as follows. Positive perception of safe management and management trust were each positively associated with policy acceptance, with a comparatively stronger association observed for management trust. Risk perception was negatively associated with policy acceptance, although this association showed some specification sensitivity when institutional trust was included in the model. The interaction between management trust and risk perception remained significant across several alternative specifications. As risk perception increased, both the management trust-policy acceptance association and the indirect association through institutional trust became stronger. In addition, these associations differed significantly across types of personal information.

These findings make three contributions. Together, these findings contribute to the growing literature on public acceptance of governmental data use under conditions of societal risk. Theoretically, the study shows that policy acceptance may be formed through two conceptually distinct pathways: an evaluation of the safety status and a performance-based judgment about the purpose of data use. It also presents a conditional structure in which this distinction varies by the boundary condition of risk perception. Empirically, the study confirms that these two pathways are independently associated with policy acceptance and shows that the management trust-policy acceptance association varies conditionally by risk level and information type. Practically, the findings suggest that, alongside the substantive quality and visibility of safe management measures, explanations of the necessity and effectiveness of data use may be associated with policy acceptance. This implication is presented cautiously, as it is based on cross-sectional data.
